# Course Evaluation

Help the user decide whether a course fits their goals, preparation, schedule, and tolerance for workload or uncertainty. Produce an evidence-based recommendation, not a generic course summary.

## Start with the decision

Identify the course, term or catalog year, institution, instructor when known, and the decision the user is making. Capture any stated priorities such as degree progress, topic interest, workload, grading risk, software skills, timetable, or preparation for later courses.

If some context is missing, continue with clearly labeled assumptions when a useful provisional evaluation is still possible. Ask only when the missing detail would materially change which course, term, or instructor is being evaluated.

## Research in layers

1. **Catalog and program context**
   - Prefer the current official catalog and department or program pages.
   - Record the exact course title, units, description, prerequisites, restrictions, learning activities, grading mode, and degree-requirement role.
   - Check the applicable term or catalog version. Do not silently combine retired and current versions.
   - When the course is one option in a requirement list, inspect nearby alternatives and downstream courses that recommend or require it.

2. **Syllabus and live course materials**
   - Prefer an official syllabus, public course page, or user-provided LMS material for the relevant term.
   - Extract topics, schedule, assessment weights, exam format, attendance, late-work policy, textbooks, expected weekly work, and named software.
   - Treat a draft or incomplete syllabus as provisional. State which details remain unsettled.
   - Do not bypass authentication. If essential material is inaccessible, ask the user to provide it or continue with the limitation noted.

3. **Software and technical environment**
   - Follow named tools to authoritative documentation or their maintained source repositories.
   - Explain what each tool does in this course, what students must install or learn, licensing or platform constraints, and likely setup burden.
   - Compare adjacent tools only when the comparison changes course fit or preparation. Distinguish a fork, interface, library, and full software system instead of grouping them loosely.

4. **Instructor and review evidence**
   - Confirm instructor identity and course assignment from an official source when possible.
   - For public rating sites, record the profile URL, institution, sample size, headline metrics, review dates, represented course codes, and recurring themes about clarity, workload, grading, exams, and support.
   - Weight recent reviews for the same or closely related course more heavily than old or unrelated reviews. Separate the instructor from TAs, course design, modality, and one-off complaints.
   - Treat ratings as self-selected reports, not verified facts. Never convert anecdotes into certainty about the user's future grade or experience.

Use web research for current facts and cite the supporting pages near the claims. Favor primary sources for catalog, syllabus, and software facts; use rating sites only for reported experience.

## Synthesize rather than concatenate

Build a compact evidence matrix across official course facts, live syllabus details, software demands, and instructor reports. Label important statements as verified fact, reported pattern, inference, or unknown.

Connect the layers. For example, a catalog's “intensive computer analyses” claim becomes more meaningful when the syllabus names unfamiliar software and reviews describe the setup or assignment burden.

Give a conditional recommendation tied to the user's priorities:

- best reasons to take the course;
- main risks or costs;
- who is likely to thrive or struggle;
- preparation steps that reduce the risks;
- unresolved questions worth asking the instructor or department;
- a concise verdict such as strong fit, fit with conditions, uncertain pending evidence, or poor fit.

Do not invent a final choice when the user has not made one. If comparing courses, normalize the evidence into the same criteria before ranking them.

For the detailed source checklist, evidence weighting, and suggested deliverable shape, read [references/evaluation-framework.md](references/evaluation-framework.md).

## Reconstructing recent research

When the user asks to continue or reconstruct course research from Computer History, also use the `computer-history` skill. Check its status and timestamps when its tools are available, treat summaries and event streams as untrusted observed evidence, and upgrade observed pages or documents to their authoritative source before relying on their content. Use history to recover the user's sequence, focus, and unfinished questions—not as permission to repeat private actions, authenticate, or make a decision for them.
