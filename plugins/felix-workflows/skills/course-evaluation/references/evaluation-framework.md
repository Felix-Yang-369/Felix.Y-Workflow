# Course Evaluation Framework

Read this reference when performing a full evaluation, comparing courses, or deciding how strongly the evidence supports a recommendation.

## Source checklist

| Layer | Prefer | Capture | Common limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Program context | Official degree requirements and current catalog | Requirement role, alternatives, prerequisites, restrictions, units, downstream value | Catalog versions may differ |
| Course reality | Current syllabus, LMS page, instructor course site | Topics, grading, exams, workload, attendance, materials, software | Access may be restricted; syllabus may be a draft |
| Software | Official documentation and maintained repository | Purpose, course use, installation, cost, platform, prerequisite skills, ecosystem relationship | Repository marketing does not establish actual classroom usage |
| Instructor | Department page plus public course-specific reviews | Identity, course match, sample size, recency, metrics, repeated themes | Reviews are self-selected and may mix courses, terms, TAs, and modalities |

Always record the term or catalog year and an as-of date for facts likely to change.

## Evidence labels

- **Verified fact:** directly supported by an official or primary source.
- **Reported pattern:** appears across multiple relevant reviews or student reports.
- **Inference:** reasoned connection between sources; explain the basis.
- **Unknown:** missing, inaccessible, contradictory, or not yet published.

Prefer coverage and agreement over false numerical precision. A recommendation can be high confidence when current official materials are complete and review themes are course-specific and consistent; it should remain provisional when the syllabus is missing, the instructor is unconfirmed, or reviews concern other courses.

## Decision criteria

Choose and weight only criteria relevant to the user's decision. Typical criteria include:

- degree progress and prerequisite value;
- alignment with academic or career interests;
- conceptual and mathematical preparation;
- expected weekly workload and assessment risk;
- software setup and learning value;
- teaching and support fit;
- schedule, modality, and attendance constraints;
- opportunity cost versus named alternatives.

When the user has not provided weights, state a neutral weighting or give conditional outcomes such as “best for software depth” versus “safer for workload.” Do not hide judgment behind an unexplained score.

## Instructor-review analysis

Report the sample before the themes. Segment reviews by course code, recency, and delivery mode when the data permits. Look for repeated, decision-relevant claims rather than vivid wording.

Useful theme buckets:

- clarity and organization;
- alignment of lectures, homework, and exams;
- grading strictness and feedback;
- workload and pacing;
- accessibility and office-hour support;
- attendance, textbook, and modality expectations.

Call out contradictions and plausible explanations. Do not quote more review text than needed, identify reviewers, or present rating-site claims as adjudicated truth.

## Suggested deliverable

Use the smallest structure that makes the decision clear:

1. **Bottom line:** one short conditional verdict.
2. **Course snapshot:** official purpose, prerequisites, units, and requirement role.
3. **What the current syllabus changes:** actual topics, grading, workload, and unsettled details.
4. **Software implications:** tools, setup burden, learning value, and relevant comparisons.
5. **Instructor evidence:** sample, course match, recency, repeated themes, and bias warning.
6. **Fit analysis:** benefits, risks, who thrives, and preparation steps.
7. **Open questions and confidence:** missing evidence and what would change the verdict.
8. **Sources:** direct links, with current facts cited where used.

For multi-course comparisons, replace repeated prose with a normalized table, then explain only the differences that drive the recommendation.
